1. Why balancing forces is not enough

$\sum\vec F = 0$ does not yet mean equilibrium: a pair of equal opposite forces applied at different points spins a body. The first condition forbids only translational motion.

§st_couple§

2. Moment of a force

§mom_arm§ $$M = F\,r\sin\alpha = F\,d, \qquad [M] = \text{N}\cdot\text{m},$$

where $d$ is the moment arm: the distance from the axis to the line of action of the force.

Two equilibrium conditions$$\sum\vec F = 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum M_{\circlearrowleft} = \sum M_{\circlearrowright}$$

The first “kills” translational motion, the second rotational motion.

§st_rotation§

Tip: choose the axis at the point where an unknown force acts; it then drops out of the equation.

3. A rod on two strings

Draw forces from their points of application: tensions from where the strings are attached, gravity from the middle of a uniform rod.

§st_strings§

A uniform rod of mass $m$ and length $L$ hangs horizontally on strings: one at the left end ($t_1$), the other at distance $l$ from it ($t_2$). Gravity acts at the middle. Moments about the left attachment and the force balance:

$$t_2\,l = mg\,\frac L2, \qquad t_1 + t_2 = mg \;\Rightarrow\; t_2 = \frac{mgL}{2l},\quad t_1 = mg - \frac{mgL}{2l}.$$

For $l = L$ we get $t_1 = t_2 = mg/2$.

4. The resultant

The school definition: $\vec R = \sum\vec F$. This course adds a second requirement: the resultant is applied at the point about which $\sum M = 0$. For a couple $\sum\vec F = 0$, but it cannot be replaced by a “zero force”: a couple has no resultant.

§st_resultant§
Detailed explanation

For a board with a load: the point of application of the resultant divides the distance between the forces $mg$ and $Mg$ so that $mg\,l_1 = Mg\,l_2$; a single support placed there is enough. This drawing is unfinished in the notebook.

The notebook has a drawing and the final formula. A board of mass $m$ lies on a table and sticks out past its edge; the centre of gravity of the board is a distance $l_0$ from the edge, over the table. A load $M$ stands on the overhanging part, a distance $x$ beyond the edge. Where is the reaction $N$ applied, and when does the board tip over?

§st_overhang§

The forces balance: $N = mg + Mg$. Let $N$ act a distance $y$ from the edge, over the table. Moments about the edge:

$$Ny + Mgx = mg\,l_0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad y = \frac{mg\,l_0 - Mg\,x}{mg + Mg}.$$
Answer

The reaction of the table cannot act beyond its edge, so the board rests while $y \ge 0$:

$$Mx \le m\,l_0.$$

5. Problems

1. A box by a wall on pulleys

To move the box, a friction force of 16 N must be overcome. The rope is pulled with 15 N. What minimum force must be applied to the box to keep it in place?

§st_box§

The pulleys transmit $2F + F = 45$ N to the box towards the wall. Friction helps with 16 N, and we supply the rest by hand: $f = 45 - 16 = 29$ N.

2. Minimum friction under a block

§st_friction_pulley§

The movable pulley is pulled down by its weight $mg/2$ and two branches of the lower string, $mg$ each. So the tension in the upper string is

$$F_\text{fr} = T = mg + mg + \frac{mg}{2} = \frac52 mg.$$

3. Where to put the pivot of a lever

$L = 60$ cm, $m_1 = 2$ kg (through a movable pulley), $m_2 = 3$ kg.

§st_lever§

The left end feels $m_1g/2$, the right one $m_2g$:

$$\frac{m_1g}{2}\,l = m_2g\,(L-l) \;\Rightarrow\; 10\,l = 30\,(60-l) \;\Rightarrow\; l = 45\ \text{cm}.$$
Correction: check the directions

In the layout as drawn the left end is pulled up ($10$ N) and the right end down ($30$ N). About any point between the ends both forces turn the lever the same way, so no pivot on the lever can balance it (the moments would cancel only $90$ cm from the left end, beyond the lever). The notebook's equation, and the answer $45$ cm, hold when both end forces point the same way. The original statement should be checked.

4. A rod with a load at a quarter of its length

§st_rod§

Moments about $B$, then about $A$:

$$T_A = \frac34 mg + \frac{Mg}{2}, \qquad T_B = \frac14 mg + \frac{Mg}{2}.$$

5. A lever of seven divisions

§st_seven§

The centre of gravity is half a division from the pivot, the string from the movable pulley two divisions:

$$Mg\cdot\frac{\ell}{2} = \frac{mg}{2}\cdot 2\ell \;\Rightarrow\; m = \frac M2.$$
Correction: check the positions

A string from a movable pulley under the ceiling pulls the lever up. With the pivot at the fourth mark and the string at the sixth, as drawn, the weight and the string turn the lever the same way. The equation balances if the weight and the string are on the same side of the pivot, for example with the pivot at the third mark and the string at the fifth. The answer $m = M/2$ assumes such a layout.

6. A light rod with loads at its ends

The length is $3a$; a string is attached at distance $a$ from the left end and connected over a pulley to a load $M = 3$ kg.

§st_two_masses§

Axis at $O_1$: $m_1g\,a = m_2g\,2a$, so $m_1 = 2m_2$. Axis at the right end: $m_1g\,3a = Mg\,2a$. Hence $m_1 = 2$ kg, $m_2 = 1$ kg.

7 and 8. A board on a table and a beam on a platform

§st_table§

7. A 5 kg board overhangs by a third of its length. We pull the overhanging end down; the axis is the table edge. The arm of the force is $L/3$, the arm of gravity $L/2 - L/3 = L/6$:

$$F\,\frac L3 = mg\,\frac L6 \;\Rightarrow\; F = \frac{mg}{2} = 25\ \text{N}.$$

Another way is to split the board at the table edge into parts weighing $\tfrac23mg$ and $\tfrac13mg$:

§st_board_split§ $$\frac{2mg}{3}\cdot\frac L3 = \frac{mg}{3}\cdot\frac L6 + F\,\frac L3 \;\Rightarrow\; F = \frac{mg}{2}.$$

8. A beam overhangs by 0.2 of its length; an upward force $F = 300$ N at the overhanging end $B$ lifts it off the platform. The axis is the far end $O$:

$$F\,L = mg\,\frac L2 \;\Rightarrow\; mg = 600\ \text{N},\quad m = 60\ \text{kg}.$$

9. When the board leaves its support

§st_sand§

The board mass is $M = 20$ kg, the supports are at $L/3$ from the left end and at the right end; the mass of the load on the left grows at $\mu = 0.5$ kg/s. At the moment of lift-off the reaction of the right support is zero. Axis $O$:

$$m g\,\frac L3 = Mg\,\frac L6 \;\Rightarrow\; m = \frac M2 = 10\ \text{kg},\qquad t = \frac{M}{2\mu} = 20\ \text{s}.$$
