1. Where friction comes from

Perfectly smooth surfaces do not exist: there is a gap between bodies, and they touch only at bumps that catch on each other.

§fric_rough§

Kinds of friction: kinetic (sliding), static, rolling and viscous.

2. Static and kinetic friction

While a body is at rest, friction adjusts to the pull: $F_\text{fr} = F$. Getting a body moving is harder than keeping it moving afterwards; this is the stick effect.

§fric_graph§ $$F_\text{fr,k} \sim N, \qquad F_\text{fr,k} = \mu N.$$

The coefficient $\mu$ depends on the materials and the state of the surfaces; $N$ is the normal force of the support.

Important addition

For static friction there is an inequality $F_\text{fr,s} \le \mu_\text{s}N$, and usually $\mu_\text{s} \gt \mu$. The formula $F = \mu N$ must not be written for a body at rest unless it is on the verge of slipping.

3. Direction

  • Kinetic friction is directed against the velocity of the body relative to the support: $\vec F_\text{fr,k}\uparrow\downarrow\vec v$.
  • Static friction is directed against the motion that would start without it. For example, on a block lying on an incline it acts up the slope.

4. Rolling friction

A wheel keeps having to “climb out” of the small dent it presses into the surface.

§fric_roll§ $$F_\text{roll} = k\,\frac{N}{R},$$

where $R$ is the wheel radius and $k$ a constant depending on the materials. The larger the wheel, the smaller the resistance.

5. Viscous friction

A body pushes the medium out of its way. The drag depends on the speed relative to the medium:

RegimeDependence
low speeds$F \sim v$
high speeds$F \sim v^2$

The drag also grows with the cross-sectional area: $F\sim A$.
